Bikepacking is cycle touring with the luxuries removed. No support vehicle, no
booked hotels, no crew handing you a bottle at the top of the climb. Everything
you will need for the next few days is strapped to the frame, and everything
strapped to the frame is weight you agreed to drag over every hill between here
and the end.

That constraint is the whole sport.

## The packing list is a forecast

A support crew lets you be wrong. Forgot the spare tube, misjudged the heat,
underestimated the climb — someone hands you the fix. Take the crew away and
your packing list becomes a prediction about the next three days that you cannot
revise once you leave.

So you start thinking in probabilities rather than possibilities. Not *could
this happen* — almost anything could — but *how likely is it, how bad is it if
it happens, and how much does insuring against it weigh?*

A spare tube is light and the failure is certain enough: take two. A full set of
tools for a repair you couldn't perform at the roadside is heavy and the failure
is rare: leave it, and accept the day it costs you. Warm layers for a desert
night are non-negotiable, because the downside isn't inconvenience.

You are, in effect, pricing risk with a luggage scale.

## Kutch teaches you about water

**Vadodara – Kutch – Vadodara** crosses some of the emptiest country in western
India. The thing that dominates the plan isn't the distance or the climbing.
It's water: where it is, how far apart those points are, and what happens if one
of them turns out to be closed.

Once you've planned a route around resupply rather than around scenery, you
understand something about supply chains that no case study teaches. The
constraint isn't total availability. It's availability *at the point of need*,
and every buffer you carry to cover the gap costs you something to carry.

That is inventory theory, learned at 30°C with a bike on your back wheel.

## The Salt Ride

**Sabarmati to Dandi** retraces the route of the 1930 Salt March. It's the only
ride I've done where the history changes the experience of the road.

You are moving under your own power along a path people once walked to make a
point, and the pace of a bicycle turns out to be about right for noticing that.
Fast enough to cover the distance in a day, slow enough to see the country
between the landmarks.

Not every ride needs a reason beyond the road being there. This one had one.

## What it's actually for

People assume the point of riding long distances unsupported is toughness. It
isn't, or at least that's the least interesting part. The point is that it
compresses a very clean feedback loop into a couple of days:

You make a set of decisions in advance, with incomplete information, knowing you
cannot revise them. Then reality tells you exactly which ones were wrong, in
order, at the least convenient moment. Then you go home and pack differently.

There aren't many places in adult life where you get that loop that fast, that
honestly, or that cheaply. It's the best planning practice I know, and it
happens to take place outdoors on a bicycle.
